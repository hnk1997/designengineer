MLP over MVP: why lovable is the better business bet

Jul 2026 · 4 min read

For years, MVP was the default. Build the smallest thing that works, ship it, learn from it. That made sense when getting anything out the door was hard and expensive, but the logic has quietly stopped serving most teams and a lot of them haven’t noticed.

The problem isn’t the “minimum” part, it’s the “viable” part.

Viable means it functions. A user can finish the task and nothing breaks. That was a fine bar when software was scarce and users were patient, but neither is true now. People have options, and when your viable-but-joyless product runs into a competitor’s version that actually feels good, viable loses. You often don’t even get the feedback you built the MVP to collect.

That’s the gap MLP fills. Minimum Lovable Product keeps the same discipline about scope but raises the bar on quality. Instead of asking what’s the least you can ship that works, you ask what’s the least you can ship that someone would be glad to use. The scope stays tight, but the care doesn’t get cut.

Viable is a low bar, and users know it

A viable product still teaches you something, but often it’s the wrong thing.

When people abandon an MVP, teams read it as a verdict on the idea. Maybe the market doesn’t want this, maybe the problem isn’t real. But plenty of the time the idea was fine and the experience just wasn’t good enough to judge it fairly, so a concept that a slightly better version would have proven out gets killed instead.

Now you’re making product calls on data your own low quality bar corrupted, which is an expensive way to learn nothing. An MLP protects the signal. When something is genuinely pleasant to use and people still don’t come back, that tells you something real, because quality is no longer the variable muddying the result.

Lovable is not the same as polished

This is where MLP gets misused, so it’s worth being clear. Lovable doesn’t mean more features, and it doesn’t mean animation everywhere or polish sprayed evenly across every screen.

It means picking the one or two moments that matter most and making those genuinely good while keeping everything else deliberately small. A booking flow doesn’t need to delight at every step, it needs to feel effortless at the exact point where people usually give up. That’s where the care goes, and the rest can stay plain.

So “minimum” is still doing real work. You’re not gold plating the whole thing, you’re being strict about scope and generous about quality only where quality changes the outcome. The hard part is knowing which moments those are, and that’s a product judgment rather than a design flourish.

Why this is a business argument, not a design preference

It’s easy to hear “lovable” and file it under nice-to-have, something designers want and the business tolerates. That framing is wrong, and it costs money.

Look at what actually moves the numbers. Acquisition gets you a user once, but retention is what makes the business work, and retention is emotional as much as functional. People come back to things that felt good and quietly leave things that felt like a chore, usually without telling you why. A viable product can acquire, but a lovable one holds onto people, and that’s where the economics live.

The cost of a weak first impression is higher than it used to be too. Attention is scarce and switching is easy, so if the first experience is only viable there often isn’t a second one. The person has already moved on, and no later improvement reaches them because they’re gone. An MLP puts the care into the moment when you can still lose someone, which is usually early.

Then there’s word of mouth. Nobody recommends a viable product because there’s nothing to say about it, while lovable is what gets mentioned unprompted, shared, brought up. That’s distribution you didn’t pay for, and it only exists above the viable line.

The tradeoff is real

Lovable isn’t free. It takes more thought, sometimes more time, and it forces harder conversations about what to cut so the things that matter can be good. That’s the actual work.

But the choice was never fast-and-cheap versus slow-and-beautiful. It’s whether you spread the same effort thin and even or concentrate it where it decides if someone stays. MVP spreads it thin and MLP concentrates it, and the second one tends to produce something people keep using, which was the whole point.

Quick answers

What is the difference between an MVP and an MLP?

An MVP asks what’s the least you can ship that works. A Minimum Lovable Product asks what’s the least you can ship that someone would be glad to use. The scope stays tight and the quality bar goes up.

Does lovable mean more features or more polish?

No. It means picking the 1 or 2 moments that matter most, making those genuinely good and keeping everything else deliberately small.

Why is an MLP the better business bet?

Retention is what makes the business work, and people come back to things that felt good. Lovable products also get recommended, which is distribution you didn’t pay for.
